# Product

## Register

product

## Users

Sato is primarily for university students, especially students working through biology, chemistry, mathematics, and other visually dense subjects. They use Sato while reading course material, reviewing diagrams, solving problems, or asking follow-up questions without leaving the app they are studying in. It also serves anyone who wants a fast, screen-aware desktop companion instead of a separate AI workspace.

## Product Purpose

Sato makes questions about visible material effortless. A user selects the exact part of their Mac screen they need help with, asks by typing or private on-device voice input, and receives a grounded explanation in a lightweight companion interface. Success means the user stays focused on the original material, understands the response, and trusts what Sato did with their screen and microphone data.

## Brand Personality

Friendly, calm, and trustworthy. Sato should feel like a capable study companion with a little warmth and personality, never like an enterprise dashboard or an attention-seeking virtual character.

## Anti-references

Avoid general-purpose AI agent cockpits, cloud-first dictation services, intrusive ambient screen monitoring, dense settings dashboards, and decorative voice interfaces that make a quick question feel ceremonial. Do not imitate chat products that pull the user away from the material they are studying.

## Design Principles

1. Keep the source material in view and ground every interaction in what the user selected.
2. Make the common path immediate, with advanced choices available only when they become relevant.
3. Treat privacy as observable product behavior, not marketing language.
4. Let the companion add warmth without competing with the user's task.
5. Keep generated questions editable so specialized scientific language can be corrected before sending.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

Support complete keyboard operation, visible focus, descriptive accessibility labels, color-independent state communication, and reduced-motion preferences. Recording, download, error, and model-readiness states must be announced in text as well as visually. Controls should remain usable for people who cannot hold a shortcut continuously, and scientific vocabulary should not assume a particular accent or dialect.
